# Tax rates: marginal, average and effective marginal

The calculators use three rates, each with one meaning, in the standard terms.

The example: a single retiree, 67, with $36,000 of Social Security, $10,000 of qualified dividends and $40,000 of Pre-tax withdrawals, $86,000 of income in all. Federal income tax is $6,467 (2026 rules). The last dollar of ordinary income is in the 12% bracket, but the next dollar also makes more Social Security taxable and pushes dividends from the 0% rate into 15%, so it costs 28.6%.

| Rate | What it is | For the example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Marginal rate** | The tax bracket: the rate on the last dollar of ordinary taxable income. | 12% |
| **Average tax rate** | Total federal income tax ÷ total income. | 7.5% |
| **Effective marginal rate (EMTR)** | The real federal income tax on the next dollar, with everything that dollar sets off. | 28.6% |

## Marginal rate

The bracket the last dollar of ordinary income falls in, from the tax tables: 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% or 37%. While income is still under the deductions, there is no taxable income yet, and the marginal rate is 0%.

It is the rate the Roth vs. Pre-tax comparison uses today: a Pre-tax contribution comes off the top of income, so it saves tax at the marginal rate.

## Average tax rate

Total federal income tax for the year divided by total income. Many sources call this the "effective tax rate"; the calculators say average tax rate, to keep it apart from the effective marginal rate. It describes the whole year, not the next dollar, so it is always lower than the marginal rate once income is taxed at more than one rate, and it is not the rate a decision about the next dollar should use.

## Effective marginal rate (EMTR)

What the next dollar really costs in federal income tax: the extra tax from one more dollar of a kind of income, with everything else in the year held as it is. Usually it equals the marginal rate. It is higher, or lower, when the next dollar also:

- pulls more Social Security into tax (the "tax torpedo": up to 85 cents of benefits made taxable with each dollar, so 12% can cost 22.2%);
- pushes qualified dividends and long-term gains out of the 0% rate into 15%;
- shrinks a deduction or credit that phases out with income (the senior deduction, the QBI deduction, the child tax credit);
- reaches the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax.

The tax page shows it for the next $100 (or the next $1,000 when the child tax credit applies, since that credit phases out in $50 steps per $1,000), and the right-hand bucket shows it at every level of income. Two things are kept apart from it:

- **Payroll tax** (FICA or self-employment tax) is real money on the next dollar earned, but never applies in retirement; the tax page shows the rate "with payroll tax" beside it.
- **Medicare IRMAA** isn't a rate: one dollar over a threshold raises the premiums two years later by a fixed amount. It is shown as a cliff, with the dollar amount.

## In the Roth vs. Pre-tax comparison

The comparison weighs the marginal rate today (what a Pre-tax contribution saves) against the effective rate on the withdrawal from those contributions in retirement: the extra tax that withdrawal causes, on top of Social Security and the other accounts, divided by the withdrawal. That rate counts everything the withdrawal sets off, like the EMTR, but spread across the whole withdrawal rather than measured on the next dollar, which is what a yearly withdrawal needs.
