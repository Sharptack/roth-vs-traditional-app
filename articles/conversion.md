# The Roth conversion calculator

A Roth conversion moves Pre-tax money to Roth: the amount is taxed this year as ordinary income, and from then on it grows and comes out tax-free, with no required minimum distributions. The calculator weighs one conversion this year, in two ways: what it costs now, and what it does over the whole plan.

## Over a lifetime, with and without it

The page runs the household's year-by-year projection twice, to the end of the plan: once with this year's conversion and once without it, everything else alike (the spending in retirement, the withdrawal strategy, the returns). Three figures decide it:

- **Lifetime tax:** federal income tax plus Medicare IRMAA surcharges, every year. Payroll tax is the same both ways, so it is left out.
- **Legacy:** the portfolio at the end of the plan, and the same after the heirs' tax on Pre-tax money (the rate set under Assumptions), since heirs inherit Roth money tax-free.
- **Total retirement income:** everything withdrawn and received in the years anyone is retired: withdrawals, Social Security, pensions, other income. Conversions stay invested, so they don't count. Spending is the same in both runs, so when the conversion lowers later taxes, less has to be withdrawn; read this figure beside the tax and the legacy.

Below them, the tax paid each year as bars, with and without the conversion, and the totals. The conversion's tax shows as a taller bar this year; lower RMDs and withdrawals later show as shorter ones.

**Who pays the conversion's tax.** Once anyone in the household has retired, the year's withdrawals cover it, as for any other tax. While everyone still works there is no withdrawal to pay it from, so the tax is held back from the conversion and less reaches Roth; the page says how much. When the money runs out in either run, the page says so: after that year spending goes unmet and less tax is paid, so the figures compare two plans that both fall short.

The lifetime view needs the household's spending in retirement, which for now is worked out from today's pay before retirement. A household already past its retirement age doesn't have one yet; the page says so (a spending input comes with the retirement spending phase).

## This year's tax on the conversion

The tax the conversion adds this year is the year's federal income tax with it minus the tax without it, so everything it drags along is counted: more Social Security made taxable, gains pushed out of the 0% rate, the Net Investment Income Tax, the senior deduction phasing out. The **effective rate of the conversion** is that tax ÷ the amount converted; it can be above the bracket when the conversion drags other income into tax. With Medicare IRMAA included in Assumptions, the page also shows the surcharge the conversion would add two years later, and the largest conversion that keeps this year's income in its IRMAA tier.

## Where the conversion lands

The ordinary brackets as a bar, filled to this year's taxable income with the conversion's part marked. Beside the top of each bracket from the current one up: the conversion that would fill it, from this year's income without a conversion. A conversion often aims to "fill" a low bracket: to convert up to its top and no further.

## What it leaves out

State tax, and the five-year rule on withdrawing converted amounts. One conversion this year; for a conversion every year, use the conversion strategies on the projection page.
